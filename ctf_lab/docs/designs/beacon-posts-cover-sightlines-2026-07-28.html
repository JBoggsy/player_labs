Design Document · 2026-07-28 · Status: Proposal, awaiting approval

Posts: cover, sightlines, and a spreading instinct near plan waypoints

A proposal to give beacon a place to fight from — position plus the sightline it watches — so plan waypoints become suggestions rather than destinations. Written for James as co-general, and for the agent who implements it. Still a proposal: implementation of the 32-direction sightline bake is underway, and nothing here has been tested or measured in a game.

Executive Summary

beacon follows battle plans literally: when the plan says pushers advance to (628, 59), five bots walk to that cell and stand on it. Measured from the v31 replay already on disk, the pushers arrive with a minimum nearest-neighbour spacing of 13 px — inside both the friendly-fire corridor and a single 52 px grenade blast — and with only 0–3 of 16 directions open past 200 px (measured on a 16-ray fan), their forward view dying in 32–108 px. The subtle part is that beacon's existing cover concept says these bots are in cover: they sat 9–22 px from a wall. The wall was between them and the threat, not beside them, because build_cover_grid asks only “is a wall adjacent” and cannot ask “is there a wall between me and the threat, with a line I can peek out along.”

This proposes one baked field and one new module. bake_map.py gains a 32-direction sightline field (free-ray distance per cell per direction, 4 px units, 400 px cap, uint8) — a measured 3.2 s to bake and 402 KiB raw (85 KiB npz-compressed), against a current nav.npz of 19,626 bytes on disk. Directional cover is derived from that field rather than baked separately: a wall beside you is just a short ray at ±45° off the threat axis. A new ctf/beacon/posts.py scores candidate cells near a waypoint on four terms — reach, cover, stance, danger — and bots claim posts over the existing shout channel with a new 6-character K message so two bots never converge on one cell. On the real arena the prototype's best post for the phase-2 push waypoint sees 400 px down the top corridor with a wall 8 px off its flank; no beacon version has ever stood there. The most consequential finding is a negative one: that best post came out 80 px behind the waypoint, so James's “ahead if pushing, behind if holding” intent does not fall out of the geometry and needs its own signed stance term.

1 · The complaint, in James's words

  • beacon obeys battle plans in the literal sense — it stands at waypoints.
  • What it does not do is understand area control, supporting fire, or sight lines.
  • The ask: a spreading instinct near a target point, onto covered cells with useful sightlines — slightly behind the waypoint if holding, ahead of it if pushing.
  • The waypoint should be a suggestion, not a destination.

The observation that started this: around frames 350–500 the pushers move up to the top lane exactly as the battle plan instructs. They arrive clumped together, neither behind cover nor looking down any useful sightline. The plan interpreter is doing its job — the bots reach the right place — and the tactical result is still worthless, because arriving somewhere is not the same as occupying it.

Two concepts beacon already partially has are supposed to carry this: cover and sightlines. Section 2 shows that one of them is measured in a way that cannot express the thing James wants, and the other does not exist in the map artifact at all.

2 · Evidence: it is real, and quantified

  • Measured from replay data already on disk — no new evaluation batch was needed.
  • Spacing at the top-lane hold: 13–47 px minimum nearest-neighbour, inside one 52 px grenade blast and well inside the friendly-fire corridor.
  • Forward visibility: 0–3 of 16 directions open past 200 px, on the 16-ray fan the measurement used; east rays dying at 32–108 px.
  • The existing cover metric passed these positions. That is the interesting failure.
  • The v25 squad spread never applied here at all, because the battle-plan rung was added later at the same altitude.

The v31 A/B arms were fetched with artifacts, so the measurement used the bundle ctf_lab/scratch/eval_v31_ab/plan_win_bundle.json directly. The episode is beacon:v31 vs ctf-focusfire:v56 on coworld ctf 0.7.91, 8v8, with beacon on the even slots (0, 2, 4, …, 14) = seats 0–7. In the active plan staged_push_top the pushers group is seats 3–7, i.e. slots 6, 8, 10, 12, 14 — the five bots every number below describes.

Those spacings need their context to mean anything. Friendly fire is ON in this game and has historically been beacon's single biggest self-inflicted cost: v2 lost roughly 6 deaths per game to its own bullets before the v3 fire gate. One grenade blast radius is 52 px — GameVersion 17 widened it from 40 px to 52 px. A group whose closest pair is 13–47 px apart is therefore sitting inside its own firing lanes and inside a single grenade's splash.

Earlier ticks are starker still. At ticks 300 and 350 all eight beacon bots are still on their spawn positions with closest pairs at 12 px, and the full alive-team pairwise spacing reads 12/12/12/12/36/36… The push does not so much spread out as travel as a block.

2.2 Sightlines: nobody chose their position for what it can see

A 16-direction free-ray fan (4 px steps, 400 px cap) was cast from each pusher's actual position. At the top-lane hold, 0–3 of 16 directions were open past 200 px. Three representative fans as measured on that 16-ray instrument, in pixels of free distance per direction, index 0 = east and counter-clockwise in screen coordinates:

  • Tick 656, slot 10 at (380, 196): [108, 308, 144, 112, 188, 12, 12, 12, 16, 24, 148, 68, 400, 52, 52, 184] — 2 of 16 open past 200 px, and neither of them points at the enemy.
  • Tick 656, slot 14 at (496, 154): 1 of 16 open past 200 px; the east-facing ray dies at 44 px.
  • Tick 756, slot 12 at (479, 156): 0 of 16 open past 200 px.
Report reach, not a direction count Every count above is measured on the 16-ray fan and is quoted at that resolution deliberately: a count of open directions is not comparable across fan resolutions. Re-cast at the design's 32 directions, both of the two cells drawn in Figure 2 read 4 of 32 open past 200 px — the same physical geometry, a different-looking number, and no more information. Future A/B reporting should therefore use forward-reach distance distributions — px of free ray along the threat axis — which are invariant to fan resolution. The 32-direction field this design bakes is what makes that the cheap reading.

One detail to expect while reading across the two instruments: the same east ray reads 108 px in the 16-ray fan above and 104 px in the 32-direction figures. That is not a resolution effect — east is index 0 in both bases — it is the one 4 px ray-stepper step now settled to stepper A (§4.3). Every 32-direction number in this document is stepper A; the §2.2 fans are left exactly as measured.

2.3 Directional cover: the subtle failure

Here is the finding that changes the design. beacon does have a cover concept, and by that concept the pushers were in cover: mapdata.wall_mask()-measured distance to the nearest wall was 9–22 px for most pushers across ticks 450–856. But their east-facing rays — toward blue, where the enemy comes from — died in 32–108 px. The wall was between them and the threat, not beside them.

That is not cover. That is a bot standing in a pocket, blind to the lane, with a wall absorbing the sightline it needed. It is the exact opposite of “behind cover with a peek angle.”

The root cause is one docstring. ctf/beacon/tools/bake_map.py:251-267, build_cover_grid: “Cover cells: walkable cells orthogonally adjacent to a non-walkable cell.” It is non-directional. It answers is there a wall near me and cannot answer is there a wall between me and the threat, with a line I can peek out along. Today nav.npz holds 1713 cover cells out of 9268 walkable cells — 18% of the walkable arena qualifies, which is roughly how much information the flag carries.

2.4 Why the plan path has no spread at all

v25 shipped a squad spread, squads.spread_point, and its A/B confirmed the fix worked for the path it was written for: stacked-ticks fell from 67 to 5.6 per appearance against h006, and from 28.6 to 5.9 against focusfire. It simply never runs on the plan path.

  • spread_point is applied only to squad ORDER points — ctf/beacon/strategy.py:154: if goal in ("H","S","P"): opos = squads.spread_point(belief.seat, opos).
  • The battle-plan rung (rung 3.9, added in v30) sits at ctf/beacon/strategy.py:190-202 and passes the raw resolved POI straight through: poi.resolve → Intent(point=xy), identical for every member of the group.

Five pushers, one target cell. That is the 13 px spacing, and it is not a tuning problem.

Lesson worth recording A fix that lands on one movement path does not cover a later path added at the same altitude. v25's spread and v30's plan rung both produce a movement target for a group; only one of them was taught to fan out. Any future group-targeting rung inherits the same hazard.

It is also worth naming what spread_point does even where it applies (ctf/beacon/squads.py:192-213): a blind rank-offset along y of 0 or ±70 px (SQUAD_SPREAD_PX), which then snaps to nearest_cover. It is geometry-blind about direction, and the grid it snaps to is exactly the non-directional cover grid from §2.3. It scatters bots; it does not place them.

3 · The concept: what a post is

  • A post is a place chosen to fight from: position and the sightline it watches.
  • Sightline is the primitive; directional cover is derived from it, not baked separately.
  • The threat axis is what makes both meaningful — the same cell is a post or a pocket depending on which way the enemy comes.
  • Spread is a consequence of post quality, not a force that pushes bots apart.

The vocabulary matters because “cover” already means something narrower in this codebase. A post is defined by three things at once: a walkable cell, a threat axis, and the free-ray distance along that axis. Change the axis and the same cell can flip from excellent to useless. This is why the design introduces a name rather than extending cover_grid — a cover cell is a property of the map, and a post is a property of the map plus the current fight.

Everything is quantised to 32 directions, 11.25° apart, index 0 = east and increasing counter-clockwise in screen coordinates. “Flank” means the axis index ±4, i.e. ±45°. Aim conversion is one multiply: index × 8 brads, from AIM_BRADS_TURN = 256 ÷ 32. The vision cone half-angle is VISION_CONE_HALF_DEG = 45° ctf/beacon/config.py, so a quantised axis can be at most 5.6° off the true bearing — an eighth of the cone.

4 · The design, in four pieces

  • One new baked field: 32-direction sightlines, measured at 3.2 s to bake and 402 KiB raw, 85 KiB compressed.
  • One new module, ctf/beacon/posts.py, owning the vocabulary and the choice.
  • Four scoring terms: reach, cover, stance, danger. The prototype ran the first two.
  • One new 6-character shout, K, so claims are knowable instead of guessed.
  • Facing commitment, so a bot watches the lane it was placed to watch.

4.1 One baked field: 32-direction sightlines

bake_map.py gains a sightline field: for each walkable cell and each of 32 directions, the free-ray distance in 4 px units, capped at 400 px, stored as uint8. Shape is 32 × 83 × 155 with GRID_H = 83, GRID_W = 155 and NAV_CELL = 8 ctf/beacon/config.py.

16 versus 32 directions is a cheap trade, and it is the reason the field is baked at 32:

Doubling the fan costs 1.3 s of offline bake time and 43 KiB in the shipped artifact, and halves the worst-case angular error against the 45° vision half-cone from a quarter of the cone to an eighth. The runtime query cost is identical — one array index either way — so the only thing 32 buys with is disk, of which this lab has plenty.

This fits the existing artifact pattern exactly. walkable, wall, cover and the four flow_{steal,home}_{red,blue} fields already bake and ship this way ctf/beacon/tools/bake_map.py:270-280, loaded by ctf/beacon/mapdata.py. The new field is a seventh entry in the same dict and one new accessor beside cover_grid().

Decision

Directional cover is derived from the sightline field, not baked separately. A wall beside you is just a short ray at ±45° off the threat axis. One field, two concepts, no redundant artifact to keep in sync.

4.2 A new module: ctf/beacon/posts.py

The module owns the vocabulary: sightline(cell, dir), cover_toward(cell, threat_dir), choose_post(belief, center, threat_dir, …). mapdata.py gains one accessor beside cover_grid() and nothing else.

The reason it is a new module rather than an addition to an existing one is ownership. mapdata.py is a thin artifact loader with no belief and no decisions; squads.py is comms and roles. Post selection needs belief — the danger field, enemy tracks, heard chat claims — plus map geometry, which is neither of those two things. It is also the unit most worth testing independently: given a wall mask, a centre, and an axis, the ranked list is a pure function, and that is a test you can write before any of this touches a game.

The prototype that produced every number in this section ran only the first two terms, with weights 0.55 × reach + 0.45 × cover where cover = 1 − min(min(flankL, flankR), 64) / 64. Stance and danger are part of the design but were not in the prototype; §4.4 is the reason stance has to exist at all.

That shape is the concept, visually.

Ray convention — settled The prototype had two ray steppers that disagreed by one 4 px step, so a cell's flank value and its published fan could differ. That is now a decision, not a discrepancy: stepper A — the free distance to the first blocked 4 px sample — is the single convention, used for the baked field and for every scoring term. Scores therefore reproduce from the artifact rather than from a re-measurement.

4.4 Why “stance” must be an explicit term

The best-scoring post for the phase-2 push waypoint at x = 628 came out at x = 548, 80 px behind it. This is not a bug in the scoring. Sightline plus cover favours the defensive side of a corridor, because that is where you get a long view with walls at your back — the geometry of a good overwatch position and the geometry of a good assault position are different, and the score as prototyped only knows the first.

So James's “ahead if pushing, behind if holding” intent does not fall out of the geometry. It needs its own signed term. This is a concrete case where building a prototype changed the design rather than confirming it, and it is the single strongest argument for shipping the stance weight as a knob rather than a constant.

4.5 Spread is a side effect, not the mechanism

The top-5 posts came out at least 56 px apart naturally: good firing positions are inherently distributed, because two cells that see the same lane from the same angle are largely redundant and the second one scores lower. That is the right causality. Bots spread because they each want a distinct good angle, not because a force shoves them apart.

Caveat If a waypoint sits in a genuinely open area there may be no good posts, and bots will bunch anyway. spread_point's blind push-apart therefore stays in place as the floor. The proposal removes it from nothing; it adds a better mechanism above it.

4.6 Threat axis: live evidence with a static prior

The axis defaults to the static prior — the direction toward the enemy pedestal, reusing action._threat_axis ctf/beacon/action.py:91-102 — and is overridden by live evidence when live evidence exists: fresh enemy tracks (Belief.enemy_tracks), the danger field's hot gradient (Belief.danger), and heard U / E shouts. Whatever the source, it is quantised to the 32 baked directions. Figure 4 shows the case that makes the override necessary rather than merely nice.

4.7 Claiming posts: a new K shout

With no comms, five bots ranking the same candidates with the same function would need pure seat-determinism to stay disjoint. James redirected this, and the redirect improved the design: chat makes “occupied” actually knowable.

The protocol reality, from ctf/beacon/chat.py:1-30: shouts are at most 10 printable ASCII characters, audible to both teams within about 247 px through walls and fog, at ±20 px jittered coordinates, one shout per second per player with one live bubble each. Two consequences follow. Earshot is free here — bots are 13–47 px apart on arrival, far inside the 247 px radius — so the real budget is the one-bubble-per-second slot, not the range.

The new message is K<seat><cell>, 6 characters, meaning “seat s is taking the post at cell,” using the existing base-36 encode_cell scheme ctf/beacon/chat.py:97-141. Arbitration becomes C > T > G > U > K > E > P ctf/beacon/chat.py:155-242: intel still wins the bubble, and the claim only outranks enemy-sighting chatter and the presence ping. It fires only while a bot is approaching or settling, is re-sent on a cadence of roughly 48 ticks, and decays after roughly 120 ticks so a dead claimant's post frees up.

Selection then reads: take the best post not claimed by a lower seat and not occupied by a visible teammate. Seat order is the tiebreak within that live check, not a substitute for it.

The v19 lesson this encodes v19's rally gate used buddy sensing under fog, so the state it gated on was permanently unknown and it deadlocked: 153 wait-ticks per agent, and wins collapsed. v20 replaced it with tick-synchronised windows. The general pattern is that a coordination check on unobservable state needs either a comms channel or a clock fallback, and v19 had neither. Posts get the comms channel, and claims still decay on a clock.

4.8 Facing commitment

A settled post stores its sightline direction, and _sweep_target ctf/beacon/action.py:105-121 centres the lighthouse sweep on the post's direction instead of the pedestal axis, with a narrowed arc. The bot watches the lane it was placed to watch. Any real target still preempts through the unchanged combat overlay ctf/beacon/action.py:446-498, and peek/duck still overrides movement ctf/beacon/action.py:304-364, so this narrows the idle sweep only.

This also finally gives the plan schema's facing field — traced-only since v30 — something real to drive.

James chose this over position-only, and his reasoning is the load-bearing part: a bot in a perfect firing position facing the wrong way is worth nothing. The rejected branches are in §7.

5 · Integration: where it hooks in

  • One insertion, inside rung 3.9, at the moment the bot is within PLAN_ARRIVE_PX = 60 px of the resolved objective.
  • The waypoint becomes the search centre — the “suggestion” James asked for.
  • Rung 3.9 keeps its altitude, so every higher rung still preempts and every lower mechanism still governs the approach.
  • The same substitution applies to order_hold / order_push and the static hold_point fallback.

When a plan objective resolves and the bot is within the arrive radius, the target becomes its chosen post rather than the raw waypoint. That is the whole integration. Because the substitution happens inside rung 3.9 rather than at a new rung, everything above it still preempts — carrying the flag, rejoin, intercepting a thief, escorting a carrier, grenade-clear, medkit, the convert trigger — and everything below still governs the approach: A* with the danger cost field, peek/duck micro, the combat overlay, windup freeze, item detours. Goalposts, not a death march, which is the v30 principle stated in ctf/beacon/plan.py:1-31.

Applying it to order_hold / order_push replaces spread_point's blind y-offset with real geometry on the path where spread already exists, and the static hold_point fallback gets the same treatment so a defender with no plan and no order still holds from somewhere it can see.

6 · Rollout, flags, validation

  • Two flags, BEACON_POSTS and BEACON_POST_FACING, so one build can A/B position and facing separately.
  • Ships with activation tracing. This is a standing lab rule, and the v7→v8 history is why.
  • Success criteria are stated in advance, in the same units as the §2 measurements.
  • A rebake of nav.npz is required.

The behaviour is gated behind BEACON_POSTS, with a separate BEACON_POST_FACING flag so the position change and the aim change can be attributed independently from a single build. Knobs live in config.py per lab convention — env-var knobs, so each iteration is attributable.

It ships with activation tracing: post cell, score, threat axis, claim source, and ticks-on-post. Every behaviour change ships with tracing because a null A/B is unreadable without it. The precedent is v7's peek-fire-duck micro, which measured null; only the v8 activation tracing showed that the mechanism did fire — duck 14.0% of alive time, peek 3.7% — and therefore was not the binding constraint. Without that trace the v7 result was indistinguishable from dead code.

7 · Alternatives considered

  • Three of the four major decisions had real alternatives, and one — claiming — was a direct redirect from James that improved the design.
  • Each rejection below is for a specific, checkable reason, not a preference.

8 · Risks

  • The largest risk is process, not code: this is a big single iteration in a lab that changes one thing at a time.
  • Two behaviours are known-unspecified: axis hysteresis, and how post facing interacts with the existing rank sectors.
  • The scoring weights are the least validated thing in the proposal.
Risk 1 · Iteration size — the one that needs a decision This is a large single iteration — bake field, new module, chat protocol change and aim change — against a lab discipline of one change per iteration. Mitigation: the two flags in §6 allow attribution without a second build. Whether that is enough is a call for James (§9).

Risk 2 · Open-area waypoints may have no good posts. A waypoint in genuinely open ground gives the search nothing to find, and bots will bunch anyway (§4.5). spread_point stays as the floor, so the failure mode is “no better than today” rather than a regression — but the scores near red_rally_top, where the top post reaches only 0.641 against 0.944 in the corridor, show this is a real regime rather than a hypothetical one.

Risk 3 · The scoring weights are unvalidated. The 0.55 / 0.45 split came from a prototype tuned by eyeball on three waypoints, and the stance and danger weights are entirely unset. These are knobs, and the first A/B may mostly teach us about the weights rather than about the concept.

Risk 4 · The K shout is audible to the enemy. Shouts carry team identity and a jittered position to both teams, so claiming a post leaks that a bot is heading to a cell. Judgement: the leak is small — ±20 px, and the bot is about to be visible in that lane anyway — and the same tradeoff was already accepted for O and P. Worth naming, not blocking.

Risk 5 · An enemy-derived threat axis can oscillate. If tracks flicker between two directions a bot could re-pick posts every few ticks, which needs hysteresis or a commit duration. The design says “settled post” but the dwell rule is unspecified — see §9.

Risk 6 · Facing commitment narrows the sweep. Trading peripheral awareness for lane focus is the point, but if a squad all commit to one axis a genuine flank could go unseen longer than today. The existing per-rank sector_offset_brads offsets partially mitigate this ctf/beacon/action.py:105-121; the interaction between sectors and post facing is unspecified — see §9.

Risk 7 · 32 directions is 11.25° of granularity. The vision cone half-angle is 45°, so a quantised axis can be up to 5.6° off the true threat bearing — an eighth of the cone. This was 11.25° at 16 directions; doubling the fan cost 1.3 s of bake and 43 KiB (§4.1) and retired most of the risk. What remains unverified is whether even 5.6° matters in play, and Figure 4 argues the axis choice dominates the axis resolution either way.

9 · Open questions

  • One question blocks starting; two need a rule chosen before implementation; one — the ray-stepper convention — has since been settled (§4.3).
Blocks starting · for James Accept the large iteration, or split the build? The two flags give per-behaviour attribution from one build, which is the argument for accepting it. The counter-argument is that a bake change, a new module, a protocol change and an aim change failing together are hard to bisect.

What is the dwell rule for a settled post? Risk 5 has no answer yet. The candidates are a minimum ticks-on-post, a hysteresis margin on the axis score, or a commit-until-the-threat-moves-N-indices rule. Something must be chosen before implementation, because the failure mode is visible thrash.

How do post facing and sector_offset_brads compose? Risk 6. The options are the post direction replacing the sector offset entirely, the offset applying on top of the post direction, or the offset applying only to bots that have no post. Each gives a different answer to “who is watching the flank,” so this is a design choice rather than a tuning one.

What are the right weights, including stance and danger? Risk 3, and the honest expectation is that the first A/B teaches us this rather than settling the concept. (The 4 px ray-stepper discrepancy that used to sit alongside this question is closed — stepper A is the stated convention for both the field and the scores, §4.3.)

Is 11.25° granularity actually good enough? Risk 7 is unverified in play, though the field is now baked at 32 directions precisely because the check was cheap offline and the change is expensive afterwards: 5.6° of worst-case error against a 45° half-cone, for 1.3 s of bake and 43 KiB (§4.1). What is not yet answered is whether reach at that resolution changes any decision the bot makes.
